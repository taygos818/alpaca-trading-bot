Meet the Taygos eight-one-eight Options Bot.

We built it for a short, aggressive paper-trading competition on Alpaca. The goal wasn't to automate a fixed watchlist. It was to search the broader U.S. market, spot unusual activity, and turn that evidence into options trades—with every decision recorded.

Here's how it works.

First, the discovery engine finds active stocks and confirms that they support tradable options. It collects price, volume, market structure, and catalyst data, then freezes that evidence before any AI agent reviews it.

Three independent agents look at the same snapshot from different angles. The technical agent studies completed bars, VWAP, opening-range behavior, trend, and volume. The catalyst agent asks whether the news or unusual activity could continue. And the macro agent evaluates the broader market—staying neutral when the evidence isn't there.

The agents can recommend a direction, disagree, or simply abstain. But they never control the brokerage account.

Deterministic code selects a call or put debit spread and checks the expiration, delta, liquidity, bid-ask width, reward-to-risk ratio, and maximum possible loss. An adversarial reviewer can object. The portfolio risk engine has final veto power.

Only an approved, signed instruction reaches Alpaca's command-line gateway. That gateway is locked to paper trading. It uses repeatable order identifiers to prevent duplicates, and it reconciles submissions, partial fills, cancellations, positions, exits, and restarts.

This dashboard is the bot's flight recorder.

The heartbeat shows whether the engine is healthy. The top-level counters summarize decisions, risk outcomes, orders, and positions. Click into an opportunity and you can reconstruct the entire chain: why it ranked, what each agent concluded, which spread was selected, what risk approved, how Alpaca responded, and whether the position exited successfully.

The activity log shows the same lifecycle in real time. And because the dashboard is read-only, observability can't become an execution backdoor.

During the competition snapshot, the bot recorded more than twenty-five thousand decision traces, made over eighty-six hundred Featherless requests, submitted sixty entries and thirty exits, and captured one hundred twenty-two option-leg fills. Alpaca reported zero rejected orders.

The financial result, though, was a loss. The account fell from one hundred thousand dollars to eighty-six thousand, three hundred twenty-one dollars and seventy-nine cents—a decline of thirteen point six eight percent.

That's not the ending we wanted. But hiding it would miss the point.

The run exposed real weaknesses: noisy catalyst attribution, excessive retries, spread execution, aggressive sizing, and a forced-flatten defect that left nine spreads open after exit limits went unfilled near the closing bell.

Taygos eight-one-eight Options Bot doesn't just show what it traded. It preserves why it acted, why it abstained, what risk approved, how Alpaca responded, and where the system failed.

That's autonomous trading—with an evidence trail you can actually inspect.
